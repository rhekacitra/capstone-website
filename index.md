## Introduction
Schizophrenia is a long-term mental health condition that affects about 1 in every 100 people worldwide. While it is often associated with hallucinations or delusions, one of its most consistent and challenging symptoms is difficulty understanding speech. Many people with schizophrenia struggle to process spoken language, especially in complex or noisy situations.

At the center of speech understanding is the auditory cortex, a region of the brain that helps us make sense of sounds, from simple tones to full sentences. Brain imaging studies have shown that this area behaves differently in people with schizophrenia when they listen to speech. However, why these differences occur has remained unclear. Newer findings show that people with schizophrenia tend to have similar auditory cortex responses regardless of hallucination status, even though their responses are generally lower than those of people without schizophrenia. 

In other words, hallucinations alone don’t fully explain why speech perception differs in schizophrenia.

Outside of schizophrenia research, scientists have found something interesting: people with higher intelligence scores often perform better on complex listening tasks. This raises an important question: **Could differences in cognitive ability, rather than symptoms like hallucinations, help explain why speech processing varies in schizophrenia?**

Surprisingly, this question hasn’t been explored much in schizophrenia research. That’s where this project comes in.

To investigate this idea, we used a publicly available brain imaging dataset from [OpenNeuro](https://openneuro.org/datasets/ds004302/versions/1.0.0). The dataset includes brain scans from people with schizophrenia and a matched group of healthy participants. While inside an MRI scanner, participants listened to three types of sounds:
- Individual words
- Full sentences
- Reversed speech, which sounds like speech but has no meaning

By comparing auditory cortex activation across different speech conditions and IQ groups, we aim to see whether cognitive differences are linked to systematic changes in how the brain processes language. Rather than focusing only on clinical symptoms, this approach looks at cognitive ability as a key source of variation. Understanding these differences could help clarify why speech perception is so challenging for some individuals with schizophrenia, and may eventually guide more personalized approaches to treatment and support.
